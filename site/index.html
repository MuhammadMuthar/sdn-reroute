Learning the Cost of Rerouting

When an SDN controller moves a big TCP flow to an emptier path, the move itself hurts that flow. We measure that damage, teach a controller to predict it, and let it move flows only when the move is worth it.

The problem in plain language

A network is a city of highways. Moving a truck mid-trip is not free.

Picture a data-center network as a city of highways. Most cars are short trips, the mice. A few are huge trucks that hog a lane for a long time, the elephants. The usual rule, ECMP, assigns each vehicle a lane by hashing its licence plate. Sometimes two trucks land in the same lane and jam it while other lanes sit empty.

A central traffic controller (the SDN controller) can see the jam and wave a truck into an emptier lane. The catch: the truck's cargo now arrives jumbled. TCP reads the jumble as loss, retransmits packets it did not need to, and cuts its sending rate. If the controller is too eager, trucks keep switching lanes back and forth and everything gets worse.

On an ONOS and Open vSwitch testbed, rerouting a TCP flow every 0.1 s cut its throughput to as low as 35% of normal, and every 2 s to about 60% (Cârpa et al., CNSM 2017). Yet the controllers that do the moving decide by path load alone. None of them knows how much a move will hurt.

What a reroute does to TCP

New packets overtake old ones. TCP calls that loss.

Reordering happens when a flow moves from a slower path to a faster one. Segments already in flight on the old path are overtaken by new ones on the new path. The receiver sees a gap, sends duplicate ACKs, and the sender retransmits and halves its congestion window. Our diagram uses the Chunk 2 delays: 5 ms on the old spine, 1 ms on the new.

Research question

How much does each reroute cost, and can a controller that predicts the cost keep the gain?

  1. How much damage does each reroute actually cause, and what predicts it? Flow rate, bytes sent so far, the delay difference between old and new path, the load on both, time since the last move, number of previous moves.
  2. Can a controller that predicts damage keep most of Hedera's gain over ECMP while causing much less of it? Two predictors: a hand-written formula and a learned model (decision tree or gradient boosting, scikit-learn, inference well under a millisecond).

The decision rule moves flow f from path p to path q only when the expected gain beats the predicted damage, and never within a cooldown of the last move.

Chunk 0 revision (pending approval). As written in the proposal, the gain side is a rate and the cost side is a segment count, so the two are not comparable. The plan now puts both sides in bytes over a decision horizon H: gain is the extra bytes f would deliver on q during H, cost is the predicted goodput deficit during recovery after the move.

What is new, and what is not

The cautious-rerouting principle is already published. The measurement is not.

We do not claim "only move when it is worth it" as new. Hermes (SIGCOMM 2017) and FlexDATE (IEEE/ACM ToN 2022) got there first. What we found nobody doing is pricing each move with an explicit per-flow estimate inside a reactive OpenFlow controller, and measuring the TCP-level harm of every policy against ECMP and Hedera.

Hermes · SIGCOMM 2017

Right rule, no controller, no damage metric

Moves a flow only when the candidate path is clearly better. Reports flow completion time only. Runs on end hosts.

Hedera · NSDI 2010

The controller, blind to disruption

Detects elephants at 10% of NIC rate, reschedules every 5 s, never counts how many flows it moves.

Cârpa et al. · CNSM 2017

Measures the harm, proposes no rule

Throughput down to 35% at 0.1 s reroutes, 60% at 2 s. Warns that the post-reroute dip can be mistaken for a need to reroute again.

Caveat we carry into the paper: the prior-work search was partial. DPLBAnt (Computer Communications 2021) and an MDPI paper on minimizing interruptions in SDN flow redirection are still unread and must be checked before any novelty claim is final.

The controllers we compare

Two baselines, one ablation, two versions of ours.

All five run through the same experiment runner on identical, seeded traffic. The cooldown-only variant was added in the Chunk 0 review so that a win cannot be credited to the cooldown alone.

1

ECMP

static baseline

OpenFlow 1.3 select groups. The switch hashes each flow onto a spine and never looks at load. Pays nothing in disruption, suffers elephant collisions.

2

Hedera-style greedy

dynamic baseline

Poll stats every 1 to 2 s. Elephant = more than 10% of link capacity. Move it to the least-loaded path. Named "Hedera-style", not Hedera: no demand estimation, no simulated annealing.

3

Cooldown only

ablation

Hedera-style plus a per-flow cooldown and a fixed hysteresis margin. No damage estimate. If this alone captures most of the gain, the paper changes.

4

Cost-aware, formula

ours

The decision rule with a hand-written damage formula built from the Chunk 5 dataset. Isolates what machine learning adds.

5

Cost-aware, ML

ours

The same rule with a decision tree or gradient-boosted model predicting the goodput deficit. Evaluated on held-out scenario groups against predict-the-mean and the formula.

One move is one flow-mod. Spines and the egress leaf forward by destination; only the ingress leaf picks the spine. A reroute is a single OFPFC_MODIFY on one switch, so there is no rule-install loss and every bit of measured damage is TCP behaviour.
Metrics

Benefit kept, damage caused, model quality, and rigor.

Benefit kept

  • Elephant throughput
  • Short-flow completion time, p50 and p99 (reported per spine class on the delay-asymmetric topology)
  • Maximum link utilization
  • AI-round completion time: synchronized rounds of equal-size elephants

Damage caused

  • Goodput deficit after each move, relative to the steady state on the new path, in bytes
  • Retransmitted, spurious and out-of-order segments per move (kernel counters via nstat first, tshark as cross-check)
  • cwnd-undo events
  • Reroutes per minute and flaps (A to B to A within a window)

Model quality

  • Prediction error on held-out scenario groups, against predict-the-mean and the formula
  • Decision time per flow
  • Closed-loop: does the model controller make better moves than the formula controller?

Rigor

  • 10 paired runs per configuration on the same seeds, means with 95% confidence intervals
  • Every number from a rerunnable script in the repo; raw results never edited by hand
  • Kernel TCP sysctls logged in every result row; ip tcp_metrics flush between runs
  • Negative results reported as measured
