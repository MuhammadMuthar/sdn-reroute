# Measure the cost of rerouting, not just the gain

## In plain language

Picture a data center network as a city of highways. Most cars are short trips ("mice"), but a few are huge trucks ("elephants") that hog lanes for a long time. The usual rule (ECMP) assigns each vehicle a lane at random by its licence plate, so sometimes two trucks end up in the same lane and jam it while other lanes sit empty. A central traffic controller (the SDN controller, here called Ryu, running on a simulated network called Mininet) can see the jams and wave trucks into emptier lanes.

The catch: moving a truck mid-trip is not free. Its cargo arrives jumbled and the driver slows down to recover. If the controller moves trucks too eagerly, they keep switching lanes back and forth and everything gets worse. Your project is a controller that moves a truck only when the time saved clearly outweighs the cost of moving it, and that waits a bit between moves.

Is it worth it? Yes, with a change of emphasis. The basic idea of "only move when clearly worth it" has already been published by others. What nobody we found has done is **measure the damage each move causes** in this kind of central controller, and use an actual estimate of that damage to decide. The recommended direction: build the cost estimate, measure the damage honestly, and test it on lopsided roads and partly broken lanes, where today's methods are weakest. That is a real, publishable-sized gap for a two-month undergraduate project.

---

**The project is worth pursuing, but not as currently framed.** The headline idea, rerouting an elephant flow only when the utilization gain beats the cost of moving it, is already published: Hermes (SIGCOMM 2017) reroutes only when a candidate path is clearly better and the flow is a good candidate to move, explicitly citing TCP reordering and oscillation, and FlexDATE (IEEE/ACM ToN 2022) makes "network disturbance" an explicit metric in SDN traffic engineering. Claiming the principle as new would sink the paper. What remains open, as far as the notes could establish, is narrower and more measurable: a reactive, centralized OpenFlow (Ryu) controller that computes an **explicit per-flow migration-cost estimate** from controller-visible statistics, applies gain > cost plus a cooldown, and **quantifies the TCP-level harm of each rerouting policy** (spurious retransmissions, out-of-order packets, throughput dip, reroutes and flaps per minute) against ECMP and a Hedera-style baseline. No opened Hedera-style or Mininet/Ryu paper reports those disruption metrics. The recommended plan is to reframe around that gap and, if the first milestone lands by week 3, extend it to asymmetric and partially failed topologies using weighted multipath, which is where recent papers openly admit they have not tested. The biggest practical risk is not the algorithm but the environment: upstream Ryu breaks on Python 3.10+, and in Mininet a reroute causes almost no measurable disruption unless the paths are given different delays.

## The cautious-rerouting principle is already taken by Hermes and FlexDATE

The closest prior work is **Hermes**, which reroutes a flow off a congested path only when all of these hold: it has sent more than 600 KB, its rate is below 30% of link capacity, and the candidate path is better by more than one hop of RTT (80 µs simulated, 120 µs testbed) and by more than 5% in ECN marking ([Hermes PDF](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~kaichen/papers/hermes-sigcomm17.pdf)). Its stated reasons are exactly your proposal's motivation: reordering triggers fast recovery and halves the congestion window, a new path's rate cannot be estimated accurately, and aggressive rerouting causes **oscillation**, demonstrated with a CONGA flow flipping between spines ([Hermes PDF](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~kaichen/papers/hermes-sigcomm17.pdf)). Hermes differs from your design in three ways that matter: it runs on **end hosts as a Linux kernel module with no central controller**, it operates at microsecond timescales, and it uses **fixed margins, not an estimated cost**. It also reports only flow completion time (10–38% better than ECMP on a symmetric testbed), not the disruption itself ([Hermes PDF](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~kaichen/papers/hermes-sigcomm17.pdf)).

**FlexDATE** brings the idea into SDN: it treats network disturbance (service disruption from rerouting) as a QoS metric, uses reinforcement learning to choose how many critical flows to reroute and linear programming to route them, and leaves the rest on ECMP. It reaches at least 90% of optimal load balancing in 99% of scenarios while cutting average disturbance by up to 9.1% and maximum disturbance by up to 38.6% ([FlexDATE project page](https://wp.nyu.edu/minghao/flexdate/); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TNET.2022.3217083)). It is an offline/RL traffic-engineering system, not a reactive per-flow rule in a polling controller, and its formal definition of disturbance was not visible in the sources.

Your baseline, **Hedera** (NSDI 2010), is the opposite pole. It detects elephants at 10% of NIC rate, reschedules every 5 seconds, and **does not model migration cost, reordering or oscillation, does not cap reroutes, and reports no count of flow moves**; disruption appears only as a qualitative remark that annealing starts from the previous state ([Hedera PDF](https://raghavan.usc.edu/papers/hedera-nsdi10.pdf)). The two remaining building blocks are also prior art. Adaptive polling is **PayLess** (NOMS 2014): per-flow timeouts start at 500 ms, double when counters barely change (cap 5 s) and divide by 6 when they jump, roughly halving OpenFlow messages (6.6 vs 13.5 per second) versus 1 s polling, but it is a utilization monitor and never drives reroute decisions ([PayLess PDF](https://rboutaba.cs.uwaterloo.ca/Papers/Conferences/2014/ShihaburNOMS14.pdf)). Safe rule transitions are **Dionysus** (SIGCOMM 2014), which schedules congestion-free updates but has no per-move cost and does not decide whether a move is worth making ([Dionysus PDF](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~jrex/papers/dionysus14.pdf)).

The evidence that disruption is real and large comes from **Carpa et al. (CNSM 2017)**, an ONOS + OVS testbed with TCP Cubic: rerouting one flow every 0.1 s cut throughput to **as low as 35%** of baseline, every 2 s to about **60%**, with the worst cases when a second reroute arrived before Cubic recovered. Moving to a longer path hurt throughput, while moving to a shorter path caused reordering with smaller impact. Crucially, the authors warn that a post-reroute throughput drop "could be mistaken for a need for further optimisation", which is precisely the feedback loop that produces oscillation ([CNSM 2017 PDF](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CNSM2017_Carpa_Assuncao_Gluck_Lefevre_Mignot.pdf)).

| Component of the proposal | Already covered by | Still open |
|---|---|---|
| Reroute only if gain clearly beats disruption | Hermes (host-based, fixed margins); FlexDATE (SDN, RL/LP) | Doing it in a reactive Ryu controller at seconds-scale polling |
| Estimated migration cost | Not found as an explicit per-flow estimate | Cost from flow rate, remaining size, RTT difference, time since last move |
| Cooldown / hysteresis | Standard control practice; oscillation shown in Hermes, Carpa | Measured flap counts with vs without it in SDN |
| EWMA / adaptive polling | PayLess | Effect of polling policy on reroute decision quality |
| Disruption metrics | Carpa (testbed, single flow) | Retransmissions, reordering, flaps per policy vs ECMP and Hedera |

A caveat on all of this: the prior-work search was partial (about 15 tool calls). MicroTE, DevoFlow, Mahout, CONGA, LetFlow, Presto and DRILL were not opened, and two directly relevant papers could not be fetched: DPLBAnt ([listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140366421003911)) and an MDPI paper on minimizing interruptions in SDN flow redirection (doi 10.3390/ijt3020010). **Read both manually before writing any novelty claim.**

## The open problem is measuring and pricing disruption in a polling controller

The genuine gap sits where the existing work stops. Hermes has the right rule but no controller and no disruption metric; Hedera has the controller but ignores disruption; Carpa measures disruption but proposes no decision rule; the recent Mininet/Ryu papers measure neither. The 2024+ **SP load balancer** (Ryu + Mininet, k=4 fat-tree, 10 Mbps links, baselines ECMP, Hedera and Sieve) claims about 15% more bisection bandwidth than Sieve but reports **no confidence intervals, no link-failure tests, no asymmetric topologies and no mice FCT**, admits its threshold elephant detection is "not precise enough", and evaluates fat-tree only ([arXiv 2601.08887](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.08887)). An IJACSA 2026 Mininet load-balancing study reports no repetitions or variance and leaves several of its own defined metrics unreported ([IJACSA Vol 17 No 3 Paper 44](https://thesai.org/Downloads/Volume17No3/Paper_44-Design_and_Experimental_Evaluation.pdf)). A survey of AI-based SDN load balancing lists "different topologies not considered", "topology changes, loss of packets not taken into consideration" and too few QoS metrics as recurring weaknesses ([arXiv 2308.02149](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.02149)).

That gives the project a concrete, defensible contribution with three parts. First, replace Hermes's fixed margins with an **explicit cost term** computed from what Ryu can see: flow rate and bytes sent (from flow stats), the RTT or delay difference between old and new path, the flow's estimated remaining size, and time since its last move. Carpa's findings tell you the sign of each term: moves to longer paths and repeated moves are expensive ([CNSM 2017 PDF](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CNSM2017_Carpa_Assuncao_Gluck_Lefevre_Mignot.pdf)). Second, separate two kinds of cost: the **transport cost** (reordering, retransmissions, window cuts), which is the novel part, and the **control-plane cost** (rule installation, transient loss), which the update literature already handles. Installing the new path before deleting the old one, in the spirit of Dionysus and consistent updates, removes the second so the first can be measured cleanly ([Dionysus PDF](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~jrex/papers/dionysus14.pdf)). Third, and most important for publication, **report the disruption** of every policy: spurious retransmissions, out-of-order segments, throughput dip after each move, reroutes per flow and flaps per minute, alongside elephant goodput, mice FCT at p50/p99, and controller messages per second.

The headline experiment then writes itself: aggressive Hedera-style rerouting gains utilization but pays in retransmissions and flapping; static ECMP pays nothing in disruption but suffers elephant collisions; the cost-aware controller should capture most of Hedera's gain at a fraction of its disruption. If it does not, that is still a publishable negative result about when disruption-awareness matters, provided the measurement is rigorous. The evaluation protocol the notes recommend is at least 10 runs per configuration with 95% confidence intervals, a fat-tree plus an asymmetric leaf-spine, mixed heavy-tailed mice and elephants rather than iperf alone, and mid-run link-down and link-degrade events.

## Four options, ranked, with what changes in the proposal

**Verdict: pursue Option 1 as the committed plan and Option 2 as the planned extension. Do not keep the proposal as is.**

**Option 1, reframe around an explicit cost model and disruption measurement (recommended core).** Novelty is medium; risk is low. The title becomes something like "Pricing the Cost of Rerouting: Disruption-Aware Elephant-Flow Load Balancing in Ryu." The existing approach section names Hedera as the algorithm being improved and cites Hermes and FlexDATE as the origin of the cautious principle. The limitation becomes "Hedera-style controllers reroute without estimating or measuring the TCP disruption they cause, and frequent reroutes cut throughput to 35–60% of baseline" ([CNSM 2017 PDF](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/laurent.lefevre/pdf/CNSM2017_Carpa_Assuncao_Gluck_Lefevre_Mignot.pdf)). The improvement is the per-flow cost estimate, gain > cost rule, and minimum dwell time. Metrics add retransmissions, out-of-order counts, reroutes and flaps per minute, and detection-plus-reroute latency to the usual throughput and FCT. EWMA/adaptive polling stays as a component, credited to PayLess, and is evaluated by its effect on decision quality (missed, late or false reroutes) rather than monitoring accuracy ([PayLess PDF](https://rboutaba.cs.uwaterloo.ca/Papers/Conferences/2014/ShihaburNOMS14.pdf)).

**Option 2, combine with weighted multipath for asymmetry and partial failures (recommended extension, best publication odds).** Novelty is medium-to-high; risk is medium. ECMP "assumes a balanced, regular, and fault-free topology"; with uneven striping or failures, identical flows get 1.66 vs 3.33 Gb/s, and weighting next hops by effective capacity (WCMP) cuts flow-bandwidth variation up to 25× ([WCMP EuroSys14](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~kaichen/courses/spring24/comp7215/papers/wcmp-eurosys14.pdf)). Wino (IFIP Networking 2025) states that "network asymmetry in datacenters is the norm rather than the exception", that partial link failures (links that slow down) "routinely occur", and leaves "failures occurring at short timescales" to future work ([IFIP Networking 2025](https://networking.ifip.org/2025/images/Net25_papers/1571127919.pdf)). The controller recomputes OpenFlow select-group bucket weights when a link degrades and applies the cost-aware rule only to elephants. The title becomes "Disruption-Aware Elephant Rerouting under Asymmetric and Degraded Topologies"; the limitation adds "ECMP and Hedera-style balancers are evaluated on symmetric, fault-free fat-trees" ([arXiv 2601.08887](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.08887)); metrics add Jain fairness index, minimum per-flow bandwidth and recovery time after a failure. No paper combining a reroute-cost model with WCMP weight updates in Ryu was found, which suggests novelty but does not prove it. The added risk is OVS select-group weight and hashing behaviour, discussed below.

**Option 3, switch angle (fallback if Option 1's disruption signal proves too weak).** Two candidates come from the notes. Early elephant detection replaces the byte-threshold trigger with a small offline-trained classifier, building on first-packet neural classification that achieved about a 15-fold flow-table reduction while covering 80% of traffic ([AGH PDF](https://home.agh.edu.pl/~jurkiew/publications/2024-05-08-elephant-flow-classification-on-the-first-packet-with-neural-networks.pdf)); novelty is low-to-medium and realistic flow-size workloads are hard to generate in Mininet. Lightweight EWMA/Holt prediction for proactive rerouting is positioned against deep RL, whose training costs 10–30× more than ECMP or heuristics and needs 1,000–5,000 episodes ([PMC12657367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657367)); novelty is low-to-medium and risk is low. Either one changes the limitation and metrics entirely (detection delay and false positives, or prediction error and proactivity), so switch only if the week-2 reordering check fails.

**Option 4, keep as is (not recommended).** As written, the proposal's core claim overlaps Hermes and FlexDATE, and its evaluation (ECMP vs Hedera on a fat-tree with throughput) is the saturated pattern shared by SP and the Hedera lineage ([arXiv 2601.08887](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.08887)). It would satisfy the course handout but would not survive a reviewer who knows Hermes. Novelty is low; risk is low; nothing changes, which is the problem.

The handout's required elements map cleanly onto Option 1 or 2: the real-world problem is elephant collisions under ECMP and the disruption caused by fixing them; the existing algorithm is Hedera-style threshold rerouting (with ECMP as the static baseline); the limitation is unpriced and unmeasured disruption, plus symmetric-only evaluation; the improvement is the cost estimate, dwell time and (Option 2) weighted multipath; implementation is Ryu on Mininet; experiments and measurable comparison are the disruption and performance metrics with confidence intervals.

## Tooling will fail before the algorithm does

**Ryu does not run cleanly on modern Ubuntu.** The last PyPI release is 4.34 from May 2020, with classifiers stopping at Python 3.7 ([PyPI ryu](https://pypi.org/project/ryu/)). On Ubuntu 22.04's Python 3.10, `ryu-manager` crashes with `ImportError: cannot import name 'ALREADY_HANDLED' from 'eventlet.wsgi'`, and the usual fix (Python 3.9 virtualenv plus `eventlet==0.30.2`) works on 3.9 ([faucetsdn/ryu #169](https://github.com/faucetsdn/ryu/issues/169)) but was reported **not** to fix the error on Python 3.10.12, with a further eventlet `TimeoutError` crash on 3.10.9 ([faucetsdn/ryu #185](https://github.com/faucetsdn/ryu/issues/185)). Ubuntu 24.04 ships Python 3.12, so neither LTS works out of the box. Three paths exist: run Ryu 4.34 in a Python 3.8/3.9 environment via pyenv or conda with pinned eventlet, which matches most tutorials and baseline repos; switch to **os-ken**, OpenStack's maintained fork (4.2.2, August 2026, Python 3.10–3.13, `pip install os-ken`) ([PyPI os-ken](https://www.pypi.org/project/os-ken)), where porting is likely an import rename but the CLI name and API differences are unverified; or use an Ubuntu 20.04 VM. Budget one to three days, then freeze the environment with a pinned requirements file or VM snapshot. A native install or VM with dedicated cores is safer than WSL, which appeared in the crash reports.

**ECMP means OpenFlow 1.3 select groups.** Install an `OFPGroupMod` of type select with one bucket per equal-cost next hop and point flows at the group; OVS picks a bucket per flow by hashing headers, so it splits by flow count, not bytes, and few elephants collide often. A Mininet example with weighted buckets (65/35) produced a 65.1%/34.9% split, but its author had to patch an old OVS (2.1) to honour weights ([smallko](https://nqucsie.myqnapcloud.com/smallko/sdn/stoachastic_switching.htm)). OVS supports several selection methods ([OVS patchwork](https://patchwork.ozlabs.org/project/openvswitch/patch/20160524034356.GA31389@vergenet.net/)), and hash-based splitting is "not perfect", with splits once differing by up to 50% between backends ([Red Hat KB 7104694](https://access.redhat.com/articles/7104694)). Verify actual weights on your OVS version with `ovs-ofctl -O OpenFlow13 dump-group-stats` before building Option 2 on them, set switches to `protocols=OpenFlow13`, fix iperf source ports or repeat many runs because collisions depend on them, and plan for ARP handling, since fat-tree loops cause broadcast storms (general practice, not from a cited source).

**Statistics polling sets your detection floor.** The Ryubook traffic monitor polls flow and port stats from a `hub.spawn` thread every 10 seconds; counters are cumulative, so rates must come from deltas, and bursts shorter than the interval are missed ([Ryubook](https://book.ryu-sdn.org/en/html/traffic_monitor.html)). Poll at 1–2 s, compute rates from the reply's `duration_sec/nsec`, and report detection-plus-reroute latency as a metric.

**Reordering must be induced deliberately, and this is the make-or-break check for week 1–2.** Without a delay difference between paths, a reroute in Mininet may cause almost no reordering and the disruption-aware benefit disappears. TCLink accepts a per-link `delay` via netem ([Mininet simpleperf.py](https://gitlab.informatik.uni-bremen.de/obergman/mininet/-/blame/5365831de864f5f83f1a86a712753ecc9766d2e4/examples/simpleperf.py)), and netem can inject reordering directly for calibration ([OneUptime netem guide](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-03-20-simulate-packet-reordering-ipv4-netem/markdown)). Use delays of at least 2 ms, and do not put netem delay on the same interface as HTB bandwidth shaping, since the netem queue distorts the shaper; IPMininet recommends netem on switch interfaces and shaping on end nodes, and notes that multi-CPU VirtualBox VMs showed netem delay swings of several hundred ms that a 1-CPU setting fixed ([IPMininet TC docs](https://ipmininet.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tc.html)). Give core paths asymmetric delays (for example 1 ms vs 5–10 ms per hop); at 10 Mbps a 1500-byte packet takes 1.2 ms, so a 5 ms gap should reorder roughly four packets, an estimate to validate because Linux TCP tolerates moderate reordering.

**Retransmissions are measurable with standard tools.** Capture with tcpdump in each host namespace and count with tshark filters on `tcp.analysis.retransmission`, `fast_retransmission`, `spurious_retransmission` and `out_of_order` ([Wireshark TCP analysis](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/wsug_html_chunked/ChAdvTCPAnalysis.html)); spurious retransmissions are the cleanest signature of reroute-induced harm. Note that Wireshark's out-of-order label depends on an RTT threshold (initial RTT, else 3 ms), so a late reordered packet can be mislabeled as a retransmission ([Wireshark TCP analysis](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/wsug_html_chunked/ChAdvTCPAnalysis.html)). Cross-check with sender-side `nstat -az TcpRetransSegs` and `ss -tin` (`retrans`, `sacked`) snapshots ([OneUptime netem guide](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-03-20-simulate-packet-reordering-ipv4-netem/markdown)). Disabling TSO/GSO/GRO on host interfaces keeps captures from showing merged super-segments (general practice). Measure mice FCT by timestamping short transfers at the client.

**Keep links slow and repeat runs.** A single machine emulating ~1 Gbps flows averaged 685 Mbps with a standard deviation of 222 Mbps once active switches outnumbered cores, while the same study reproduced Hedera beating ECMP on a k=4 fat-tree at about 12 vs 10 Mbps bisection bandwidth ([Wu et al., WSC 2017](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc17papers/includes/files/029.pdf)). Use 10 Mbps links, confirm an idle iperf reaches about 95% of configured bandwidth, run `mn -c` between runs, and log software versions. For baselines, Huangmachi/exp_BFlows bundles Ryu ECMP and Hedera on a k=4/k=8 fat-tree with iperf and plotting scripts, but appears to be Python-2-era and needs porting and a license check ([GitHub exp_BFlows](https://github.com/Huangmachi/exp_BFlows)).

## Conclusion

The shift in understanding is that the project's value lies in the measurement, not the rule. "Reroute only when it is worth it" is settled as a principle; what the field lacks is evidence of how much a centralized controller's reroutes actually cost TCP and a cost estimate grounded in what that controller can observe. Hedera never counted its moves, Hermes never measured its disruption, and current Mininet/Ryu papers test only symmetric, fault-free fat-trees. A team that prices disruption explicitly, measures it with confidence intervals, and shows where the trade-off flips under asymmetry and partial failure is filling a gap that reviewers will recognise, rather than restating Hermes in Python.

The schedule should follow the risks. Freeze the environment in week 1, prove in week 2 that a reroute between unequal-delay paths produces countable spurious retransmissions (if not, move to Option 3), deliver the Option 1 comparison by week 5, and attempt Option 2's weighted multipath only after verifying OVS select-group weights on the installed version. Before submission, read DPLBAnt and the MDPI flow-redirection paper, the two sources most likely to undercut the novelty claim.
